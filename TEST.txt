TEST QUERIES - Assignment 3
===========================

These are the 25 queries used to evaluate the search engine. The same list, in
machine-readable form, is in queries.txt; "python evaluate.py" runs all of them
and logs the top 5 results and the time of each query.

Each query was first run on the Milestone 2 ranking (the baseline) and tagged
GOOD if the top results were what a person would expect, or BAD if they were
not (the same tags are used in queries.txt and the evaluate.py log). The
changes made afterwards, and their effect on each query, are below.

PART 2 - QUERIES THAT STARTED BY DOING POORLY
---------------------------------------------

 13. machine learning
 14. phd program
 15. how to apply
 16. to be or not to be
 17. the university of california irvine
 18. faculty
 19. privacy
 20. contact us
 21. computer science
 22. ics 31
 23. graduate admissions
 24. game design
 25. human computer interaction

The problems with the baseline fell into three groups:

  a) News archive and listing pages (for example /2017/08/ or /news/page/22/)
     ranked above real pages. These pages are very long and contain many
     different words, so they match almost any query and collect a lot of score.
     Affected: machine learning, phd program, the university of california
     irvine, ics 31, game design, human computer interaction.

  b) All "important" text (title, headings, bold) shared one weight, so a word
     in a page's title counted no more than a bold word in its body.
     Affected: contact us, computer science, graduate admissions.

  c) Words that appear on almost every page (faculty, privacy, computer science)
     have an idf near 0, so their scores were near 0 and the order of the
     results was close to arbitrary.
     Affected: faculty, privacy, computer science, graduate admissions.


PART 3 - WHAT WAS CHANGED TO MAKE THEM BETTER
---------------------------------------------

CHANGE 1: Separate weights for title, headings and bold text

  Before, each posting stored one "important_tf". Now the index stores three
  separate counts: title_tf, heading_tf (h1-h3) and bold_tf (b, strong). A word
  inside several of these counts once, as the strongest kind
  (title > heading > bold). A term's weight in a page is now

      (1 + log tf) + 3 * (1 + log title_tf) + 2 * (1 + log heading_tf)
                   + 1.5 * (1 + log bold_tf)

  (a kind with a count of 0 adds nothing), multiplied by idf.

  Why: a page's title is a much better summary of the page than a bold word
  in its body. This is a general rule, not tuned to any one query.

  Helped:
    - machine learning: the news listing pages left the top 5; all top 5 are
      now articles about machine learning.
    - privacy, faculty: pages that have the word in their title (pages ABOUT
      the topic) now beat pages that only have it in the footer. This also
      fixes problem (c) for these words: even with a tiny idf, the title bonus
      separates the relevant pages from the rest.
    - contact us: the contact page is now #1.
    - computer science: the CS home page went from #2 to #1.
    - game design, human computer interaction: the archive pages are gone from
      the top 5.
  Hurt:
    - ics 31: the course list fell from #1 to #2.
    - graduate admissions: the application page fell from #2 to #3.
    - running: see Part 1.
  Cost: index.json grew from 13.8 MB to 21.9 MB; loading it takes about 0.05 s
  longer.

CHANGE 2: Length normalization

  A page's score is now divided by (its vector length) ** 0.5, where the vector
  length is sqrt(sum of the squared weights of all its terms). The lengths are
  computed once when the search engine starts, never during a query.

  Why: problem (a). Long pages match more queries and collect more score just
  because they contain more words.

  Full cosine normalization (dividing by the whole length, power 1.0) was tried
  first. It removed the archive pages but also pushed useful long pages down:
  the CS home page fell from #1 to #4 for "computer science" and the course
  list left the top 5 for "ics 31". The square root (power 0.5) keeps most of
  the gains with much less damage, so that is what is used.

  Helped:
    - the university of california irvine: the two archive pages at #2-#3 are
      gone (one archive page remained at #5; it is no longer in the top 5
      after all the changes).
    - faculty: the faculty profiles directory is now #3; it was not in the top
      5 before.
    - mswe: the graduate overview page replaced a news listing page.
  Hurt:
    - computer science: the CS home page moved from #1 to #2, behind the
      graduate CS programs page (still a reasonable answer).
    - ics 31: the course list moved from #2 to #3.
    - graduate admissions: the application page moved from #3 to #4.

CHANGE 3: Speed

  The top k results are now picked with a heap (heapq.nsmallest) instead of
  sorting every matching page. The slowest of the 25 test queries now takes
  about 4 ms, well under the limit.


CHANGE 4: Dotted abbreviations (Ph.D. -> phd)

  When groups of one or two letters are joined by periods (Ph.D., U.S., U.S.A.),
  the tokenizer keeps the separate pieces ("ph", "d") and also adds the joined
  word ("phd"). Groups longer than two letters (www.ics.uci.edu) and numbers
  (3.14) are not joined. The same tokenizer is used for queries.

  Why: pages write "Ph.D." but people type "phd", so the query never matched the
  PhD program pages. The pieces are kept so every alphanumeric sequence is still
  a token, as the assignment requires.

  Helped:
    - phd program: the Ph.D. in Informatics and Ph.D. in Software Engineering
      pages are now #1 and #2 (they were not in the top 5).
  Side effect:
    - contact us: "U.S." now also gives "us". The contact page is still #1, but
      #2-#5 are now articles about U.S. News rankings.

CHANGE 5: Word pairs (2-grams)

  The indexer also saves every pair of neighboring words on each page, in a
  second file (ngrams.json). When a query has two or more words, each pair of
  neighboring query words is looked up, and pages that contain the same pair
  get a bonus: idf of the pair * (1 + log tf of the pair) / page length.

  Why: plain tf-idf treats a page as a bag of words and ignores word order. A
  news archive page with "ICS" in one story and "31" in another scored like the
  course list that says "ICS 31". Pages that have the query's words side by
  side are much more likely to be about the query.

  Word pairs that appear on more than 25% of all pages get no bonus. These are
  menu and footer text: "Graduate Admissions" is a link in the CS site menu on
  31% of pages. Without this rule, very short pages that only carry the menu
  took #1 for "graduate admissions".

  Helped:
    - ics 31: the course list is now #1 and the profile of the professor who
      teaches ICS 31 is #2. Before, a news archive page was #1.
    - how to apply: the undergraduate application page contains "how to" and
      "to apply" side by side, and is now #1. Before, every top-5 result was
      an archive page or an article about "applied" research.
    - phd program: the program pages contain the pair "phd program", which
      keeps them at #1 and #2 together with change 4.
  Cost: ngrams.json is 18.4 MB, and loading the index takes about 0.5 s
  longer when the search starts.

CHANGE 6: Anchor text

  The words of each link (the text between <a href="..."> and </a>) are added
  to the page the link points to, as a separate count (anchor_tf, weight 2).
  Links to pages outside the dataset, and links from a page to itself, are
  ignored.

  Why: the words other pages use to link to a page describe that page well.
  Many pages link to the faculty directory with the word "Faculty", even though
  the directory itself does not repeat the word often.

  (The effect below was checked by running the queries again with the anchor
  text weight set to 0.)
  Helped:
    - faculty: the faculty directory is now #1 (it was #3), and the CS faculty
      page entered the top 5 at #4.
    - human computer interaction: the MHCID program page moved from #2 to #1.
  Hurt:
    - computer science: the CS home page moved from #2 to #4, behind the two
      BS degree pages for Information and Computer Science and Computer Game
      Science (still reasonable answers).
    - how to apply: the "Applied Innovation" article moved up to #2 (the
      application page stays #1).

CHANGE 7: Removing exact duplicate pages

  For each page, a hash (SHA-256) of all its words in order is computed. If a
  later page has the same hash, it is skipped and listed in skipped.json as
  "duplicate content". Links to the skipped copy's URL count for the kept page.

  Why: the same page sometimes exists under two URLs (for example /mars/ and
  /mars/index.shtml), and both copies took places in the top 5.

  Result: 37 pages were removed (1,212 -> 1,175 indexed pages). For
  "information retrieval" the second copy of the MARS page left the top 5.

CHANGE 8: Three-word phrases (3-grams)

  ngrams.json now also holds every run of three neighboring words on each page.
  When a query has three or more words, each run of three neighboring query
  words is looked up, and pages that contain the same three words in the same
  order get the same kind of bonus as a word pair in change 5. The 25% rule
  from change 5 applies to 3-grams too.

  Why: word pairs only check two words at a time. A page that says "how to"
  in one sentence and "to apply" in another got the same bonus as a page that
  says "how to apply". A 3-gram only matches when the whole phrase is there.

  Helped:
    - how to apply: the graduate application page entered the top 5 at #4.
      The "Applied Innovation" article and a news listing page (/news/page/19/)
      left the top 5. The undergraduate application page stays #1.
    - On other long queries tried during testing (for example "neural network
      deep learning classification"), news listing pages left the top 5.
  Hurt:
    - None. The other 24 queries have exactly the same top 5 as before.
  Cost: ngrams.json grew from 18.4 MB to 42.3 MB, and loading the index at
  startup takes about 3 s instead of 1.5 s. The slowest query still takes
  under 5 ms.

CHANGE 9: PageRank

  The indexer runs PageRank (damping factor 0.85) over the links between the
  indexed pages and saves the result in pagerank.json. Each link from one page
  to another counts once, and links from a page to itself are ignored (these
  are the same links used for the anchor text in change 6). At search time,
  each page's score is multiplied by

      1 + 0.3 * log(1 + rank * N) / log(1 + top_rank * N)

  where N is the number of pages and top_rank is the highest PageRank. The
  best-linked page gets at most a 30% boost. If pagerank.json is missing, the
  search still works, with no boost.

  Why: problem (c). Words that are on almost every page (faculty, research,
  about, undergraduate) have scores near 0, so the page that everyone links to
  (usually the menu page for that topic) did not stand out from the rest.
  Because the boost multiplies the score instead of adding to it, it breaks
  near-ties but cannot lift a weak match above a much better one.

  Tuning: weights from 0.1 to 0.5 were tried. 0.3 is the lowest weight that
  brings the menu pages that were checked (cs/faculty/,
  cs/about-the-department/, BS Computer Game Science) into the top 3. From 0.4
  up, menu pages (housing, student groups, contact) show up in unrelated
  queries such as "the university of california irvine".

  Helped:
    - faculty: the CS faculty page moved from #4 to #2.
    - game design: the BS in Computer Game Science page moved from #8 to #2.
    - how to apply: the graduate application page moved from #4 to #3.
    - graduate admissions: the CS graduate programs page entered the top 5
      at #5.
    - Extra queries tried during testing: "about" (the CS About the
      Department page went from #7 to #1) and "research" (the CS research
      centers page went from #2 to #1).
  Hurt:
    - human computer interaction: the course list moved from #6 to #4.
    - phd program: #4 and #5 are now the graduate policies and diversity
      ambassador pages instead of PhD student stories.
    - A few news stories that were in the CS "recent news" sidebar when the
      site was crawled are linked from many pages, so they have a high
      PageRank and move up a place or two.
  Unchanged: 24 of the 25 test queries keep the same #1 result ("machine
  learning" swaps its #1 and #2, both on topic), and 13 keep exactly the same
  top 5.
  Cost: computing PageRank over the 88,970 links adds 0.5 s to the index
  build. The boost adds about 0.02 ms per query; the slowest test query takes
  about 4 to 6 ms.
